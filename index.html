The number this is built on

1,026

Children aged 10–18 robbed on the streets of Los Angeles during school commute hours between 2020 and 2024. That is 56% of all 1,838 robberies of kids in that age range — more than half, concentrated into about five hours of the day.

It is not random. It repeats, and it repeats in specific places that are already in the public record. Nobody routes around them.

What the data showed us

Splitting incidents by time of day only means something if you divide by how long each window lasts — "after dark" is 12 hours and would look worst just for being longest. Per hour:

Morning 05–102,052
Afternoon 10–174,246
After dark 17–053,804

The walk home is the most dangerous hour of a student's day — worse, hour for hour, than after dark. That is exactly the 2–6 p.m. window the robbery figure points at, and we did not go looking for it. It fell out of the data.

Why this is a transportation problem

The federal Safe Routes to School program has existed since 1971 and is almost entirely about cars — crosswalks, speed bumps, crossing guards. But ask an LA teenager what they are actually afraid of on the walk home and they will not say traffic.

Walking is the most basic mode of transport there is, and for a lot of students it is the only one they have. When the walk stops feeling safe, kids stop walking — which pushes them into cars, onto worse schedules, or into missing school entirely. Making the walk safer is transportation work.

Why it scales globally

Nothing here is specific to Los Angeles except one bounding box in config.py. The pipeline needs three public inputs: geocoded incident reports, school locations, and OpenStreetMap — and OSM already covers the entire planet.

Hundreds of cities publish compatible open incident data on Socrata and ArcGIS portals, including Chicago, New York, Seattle, Denver, Austin, Baltimore, Toronto and London. Pointing this at any of them is a configuration change, not a rewrite. There is no backend and no per-user cost, so a city can stand up its own copy for the price of static hosting: nothing.

What we filter out, and why

The raw LAPD feed has roughly a million records. We keep only incidents that describe a threat to someone walking down a street — robbery, assault, ADW, criminal threats, brandishing. Property crime and fraud are dropped: a stolen catalytic converter says nothing about whether a child is safe.

Then a premise filter keeps only street, sidewalk, alley, bus stop, park and underpass. This drops about 81,000 incidents that happened indoors. Domestic violence is real and serious, but it is not a walking-route hazard, and including it would systematically mislabel dense residential neighbourhoods as dangerous to walk through.

The risk model

Each surviving incident is weighted severity × juvenile × recency. Armed robbery counts 2.5×, assault with a deadly weapon 3×, a verbal threat 1×. Recency decays exponentially on a 2.5-year half-life.

Crimes against children count triple. A crime against a 13-year-old predicts danger to a 13-year-old far better than a crime against an adult does — that weighting is the single biggest thing separating this from a generic crime map.

Incidents are split into three time buckets and spread as Gaussian kernels with a 120 m bandwidth, chosen to match the block-level uncertainty in LAPD's geocoding rather than to imply precision we do not have. Every block is sampled every 25 m, then rank-normalised — so 0.9 means "worse than 90% of blocks in LA."

Streetlight density earns a block up to a 35% reduction, capped so a well-lit block in a high-crime area never reads as safe. Arterials take a penalty; footpaths get a small credit.

The routing

Streets are cut into blocks at genuine intersections — shared OSM node IDs, not rounded coordinates — and reduced to the largest connected component. Routing is A* minimising:

length × (1 + λ · risk1.5)

λ is the slider. The 1.5 exponent tolerates mildly-elevated blocks while avoiding genuinely bad ones hard; a linear penalty gives mushy routes that dodge everything slightly and nothing much.

Because every edge multiplier is at least 1, straight-line distance never overestimates the remaining cost — the heuristic is admissible, so the route returned is provably optimal, not approximate. It all runs in your browser: the graph is precomputed offline, so there is no server to pay for.

Honest limitations

  • Reported crime is not all crime. Reporting rates vary by neighbourhood and immigration status, so a low score can partly reflect low reporting.
  • Locations are block-level. LAPD rounds coordinates for privacy; the 120 m bandwidth matches that uncertainty.
  • Data ends December 2024, when LAPD migrated to NIBRS. Complete and stable, but not live.
  • Risk is relative to LA. A 0.2 means "quiet by LA standards," not "quiet."
  • This is a second opinion, not a guarantee. An empty street the model likes may still be worse than a busy one it doesn't.
