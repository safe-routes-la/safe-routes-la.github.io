    The problem

    1,026
    Children aged 10 to 18 robbed on the streets of Los Angeles during school commute hours between 2020 and 2024. That is 56% of all 1,838 robberies of children in that age range, inside about five hours of the day.

    Those robberies repeat in specific places, and every one of those places is already in the public record. What no map app does is route around them.

    The federal Safe Routes to School program dates to 1971 and deals almost entirely with cars: crosswalks, speed bumps, crossing guards. Ask a student in Los Angeles what worries them on the walk home and traffic is rarely the answer.

    What we measured

    Splitting incidents by time of day only means something once you divide by how long each window lasts. Evening covers 12 hours and would look worst just for being longest. Per hour:

    The afternoon walk home is the worst hour of a student's day, worse hour for hour than after dark. That is the same 2pm to 6pm window the robbery figure points at. We were not looking for it.

    Where the data comes from

    • 85,634 violent incidents from the LAPD crime dataset, 2020 through 2024.
    • 128,534 streetlights from the LA Bureau of Street Lighting.
    • 668 schools from the California Department of Education directory.
    • 18,473 km of walkable street from OpenStreetMap, cut into 431,599 blocks.

    What we threw away

    The raw LAPD file holds roughly a million records. We keep only offences that threaten someone walking down a street: robbery, assault, criminal threats, brandishing a weapon. A stolen catalytic converter says nothing about whether a child is safe on that block.

    A second filter keeps only incidents that happened in public space, meaning street, sidewalk, alley, bus stop, park, underpass. That one drops about 81,000 records that happened indoors. Domestic violence is serious and it is also not a hazard of walking past a building, so counting it would have marked ordinary residential blocks as dangerous to walk through.

    How a block gets scored

    Every surviving incident is weighted by how severe it was, how recently it happened (exponential decay on a 2.5 year half life), and whether the victim was a child. Crimes against children count triple, because a crime against a 13 year old tells you more about the risk to a 13 year old than a crime against an adult does.

    Incidents then spread out as Gaussian kernels with a 120 m bandwidth. We picked 120 m to match how coarsely LAPD records locations, not to suggest we know more precisely than that. Each block is sampled every 25 m and scored, then ranked against every other block, so 90 means the block is worse than 90% of Los Angeles at that hour.

    Streetlights earn a block up to 35% off its score, capped so a well lit block in a bad area never comes out looking calm. Wide arterials take a penalty, footpaths get a small credit.

    How the route is chosen

    Routing is A*, minimising length × (1 + λ · risk1.5). The three options you get are the same search at three values of λ. The 1.5 exponent tolerates slightly elevated blocks while avoiding genuinely bad ones firmly. A linear penalty gives mushy routes that dodge everything a little.

    Every edge multiplier is at least 1, so straight line distance never overestimates what is left to walk. The heuristic is admissible and the route is provably the cheapest one, not an approximation. validate.py checks this against Dijkstra on random pairs and the costs come out identical.

    All of it runs in this browser. The scored graph is built offline and shipped as a 5 MB binary, so there is no server behind this page and no cost to running it.

    Where it runs next

    One bounding box in config.py is the only Los Angeles specific thing here. The pipeline needs geocoded incident records, school locations, and OpenStreetMap, which already covers the planet. Chicago, New York, Seattle, Denver, Austin, Baltimore, Toronto and London all publish incident data in a compatible shape, so pointing this at one of them is configuration rather than a rewrite.

    What this cannot tell you

    • Reported crime is not all crime. Reporting rates vary between neighbourhoods and with immigration status, so a low score partly reflects who calls the police.
    • Locations are approximate. LAPD rounds coordinates to protect victims, which is why the model smooths over 120 m.
    • The data stops in December 2024, when LAPD moved to a new records system. It is complete and stable, and it is not live.
    • Scores are relative to Los Angeles. A 20 means quiet by the standards of this city.
    • This is a second opinion about a walk. An empty street the model likes can still be worse than a busy one it marks down, and you know things about your own neighbourhood that a crime table does not record.
